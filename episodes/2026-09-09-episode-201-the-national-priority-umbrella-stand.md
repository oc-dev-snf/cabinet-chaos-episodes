# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 201
**Title:** The National Priority Umbrella Stand
**Date:** 2026-09-09
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The Minister enters the Cabinet room and stops.

Beside the door is an umbrella stand containing no umbrellas.

Instead, it holds rolled-up strategies, three coloured batons, a traffic wand, and a laminated tag reading **PRIORITIES FOR IMMEDIATE WEATHER**.

"Why is there a policy bucket by the door?"

The Permanent Secretary corrects him without looking up.

"It is the National Priority Umbrella Stand."

"That is worse."

The comms director is already deciding whether it photographs better from the left.

"It says preparedness."

"It says we have moved from governing by tray to governing by cloakroom."

The policy lead gestures to the batons.

"Each department places its top priority in the stand so the centre can see what is ready to be carried forward."

"Carried where?"

There is a pause.

"Forward."

The junior official arrives with a clipboard.

"Facilities would like to know whether the stand is for dry priorities only."

The Permanent Secretary nods gravely.

"We may need a wet-policy annex."

## ACT ONE

The umbrella stand began as a solution to overcrowded meeting tables.

By nine o'clock it had become a whole-of-government prioritisation architecture.

By half past nine it had a diagram.

By ten it had been described as "a vertical delivery environment" by a consultant who had never successfully folded a map.

The Minister reads the note.

"The stand allows priorities to remain upright while awaiting ministerial grip."

"That means we are propping them up," says the junior official.

"Only temporarily," says the Permanent Secretary.

"Everything here is temporary until it becomes furniture."

Legal is worried that anything placed in the stand may be deemed to have entered active consideration.

Finance is worried that anything in active consideration may need a spending line.

Comms is worried that the empty hooks imply spare capacity.

"Could we add some decorative pressure?" asks the comms director.

"No," says the Minister. "We are not dressing a policy failure with bunting."

The policy lead holds up a baton.

"This one represents public confidence."

"Why is it damp?"

"It came from Transport."

## ACT TWO

Departments begin submitting priorities in unsuitable shapes.

Health sends a rolled-up waiting list plan too wide for the stand.

Education sends a ruler labelled **LONG-TERM CERTAINTY**.

Treasury sends an envelope saying it supports the stand provided nobody mistakes it for storage, endorsement, acceleration, or a promise.

The Permanent Secretary convenes the Uprightness Panel.

The Minister stares at the agenda.

"Item one: agree whether leaning counts as progress."

"In some delivery contexts," says the policy lead, "leaning is a precursor to movement."

"So is falling over."

The comms director suggests a launch slogan: **Holding Priorities Together**.

Legal says that may imply the priorities have been separated.

Finance says separation may be cheaper.

The junior official notices one of the batons has no label.

"Whose priority is this?"

Everyone looks at it.

The Permanent Secretary says it may be cross-cutting.

"It is a stick," says the Minister.

"A cross-cutting stick."

Comms writes that down, then slowly crosses it out.

## ACT THREE

By lunchtime the stand has become a queue.

No department wants its priority at the back, so each argues that its item is not in the stand but "strategically adjacent to readiness."

The corridor fills with officials holding rolled papers at careful angles.

The policy lead unveils a colour code.

Green means a priority is upright.

Amber means a priority is supported by another priority.

Red means a priority has slipped behind the stand and is now being considered through retrieval governance.

The Minister points at the floor.

"There is a folder under it."

The junior official kneels down.

"It says 'urgent decision on simplification.'"

"How long has it been there?"

The Permanent Secretary checks the log.

"Since the pilot phase."

"Of what?"

"Simplification."

The comms director says the public should not see ministers pulling plans out from behind furniture.

"The public have seen worse," says the Minister.

"Yes, but usually at a distance."

The policy lead suggests an emergency stand reset.

Legal asks whether resetting the stand resets the priorities.

Finance asks whether reset priorities need fresh baselines.

The Minister says he is going to baseline the window open.

## CLIMAX

In the press room, the umbrella stand stands beside the lectern like an object waiting to be denied.

The Minister faces the cameras.

A reporter asks whether the government is now ranking national priorities by where they fit in hallway equipment.

The comms director smiles with the expression of someone trying to land a plane using a stapler.

The Permanent Secretary gives the smallest nod ever recorded by public administration.

The Minister lifts the unlabelled baton.

"A priority is not serious because it has a label, a colour, a place in a stand, or a nice phrase around it. It is serious when somebody is responsible for doing it, by a date, with the means to do it, and the courage to say what comes second."

The room goes still.

The junior official passes him the old simplification folder from under the stand.

The Minister opens it.

Inside is one page.

At the top it says:

**DO THREE THINGS. STOP PRETENDING EVERYTHING IS FIRST.**

He signs it.

With an ordinary pen.

## TAG

Back in the Cabinet room, the umbrella stand contains two umbrellas.

One belongs to the junior official.

The other is labelled **CONTINGENCY**.

The Minister takes it.

"Finally," he says, "a policy I can understand."

**END**
