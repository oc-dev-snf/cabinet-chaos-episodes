# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 210
**Title:** The National Urgency Timer
**Date:** 2026-09-18
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The Minister enters the briefing room to the sound of ticking.

On the table sits a small kitchen timer shaped like a tomato.

"Why is the meeting being threatened by a salad?"

The Permanent Secretary adjusts his papers without looking at it.

"It is the National Urgency Timer."

"It is for boiling eggs."

"It is for visibly managing pace."

The comms director explains that the public need to see government moving at speed, but in a controlled, reassuringly circular way.

"So we bought a tomato."

"We procured a time-visible delivery aid."

The policy lead winds it carefully.

Across the front, someone has stuck a label:

**ACTION IN PROGRESS.**

The timer immediately begins ticking louder than the Minister.

"Is it supposed to do that?"

"Yes," says the Permanent Secretary. "It creates ambient grip."

The junior official writes down "ambient grip may require decibel handling."

"Do not minute the tomato."

"I minuted it as a pressure indicator."

## ACT ONE

The timer began as a way of keeping the morning grid meeting under twenty minutes.

By half past nine, it had become a cross-government framework for demonstrating urgency without creating deadlines.

The Minister reads the submission.

"Departments are invited to identify suitable countdown moments across priority activity."

"What is a countdown moment?"

"Any occasion on which something must appear to be approaching."

The comms director has prepared three official timer settings.

Five minutes means rapid grip.

Ten minutes means considered acceleration.

Fifteen minutes means urgent reflection.

The Minister looks up.

"Urgent reflection is sitting still faster."

"Only if poorly chaired," says the Permanent Secretary.

Legal asks whether a timer going off could be construed as a commitment.

The policy lead proposes calling the bell a "non-binding audio milestone."

The comms director likes it, but says "bell" feels too festive.

The Permanent Secretary suggests "culmination tone."

Nobody is happy, which means it survives.

## ACT TWO

Departments begin requesting specialist timers.

Transport wants one that can run late while remaining on schedule.

Health asks for a wipe-clean timer to support visible resilience.

Education proposes a silent timer, then worries it may imply invisible urgency.

The Treasury asks whether existing clocks can be reclassified as legacy timers.

The policy lead unveils the Urgency Calibration Wheel.

Green means prompt.

Amber means imminent.

Red means active imminence.

Purple means urgent but not today.

The Minister points at purple.

"Why is purple on an urgency wheel?"

"For decisions that must be seen to be near without becoming close."

The comms director receives a message from the press office.

A photograph has emerged of a timer sitting next to an untouched stack of overdue correspondence.

The headline is already bad.

The Permanent Secretary says the key is to distinguish between unmanaged delay and structured waiting.

"The letters were ignored."

"In a timed environment."

The junior official notes that the tomato stopped ticking three minutes ago.

Everyone looks at it.

For the first time all morning, the room is perfectly quiet.

## ACT THREE

An emergency pace board is convened to decide whether the timer should feature in the afternoon announcement.

The meeting starts late because nobody can agree when to wind it.

The comms director proposes placing the timer beside the lectern.

"It gives the announcement momentum."

"It gives the announcement a threat," says the Minister.

The Permanent Secretary says the object should be present but not dominant.

Facilities offers to put it behind a folder.

The policy lead says hidden urgency is contrary to the framework.

The junior official suggests using a normal clock.

The room treats this as though she has proposed abolishing Tuesday.

"A clock tells the time," says the Permanent Secretary.

"Yes."

"This tells a story about the time."

The Minister picks up the tomato.

"The story is that we have confused haste with an ornament."

The timer rings.

Everyone jumps.

The junior official says, very quietly, "That may be the first outcome."

## CLIMAX

The afternoon announcement is staged in a room with a lectern, three flags, and one carefully positioned National Urgency Timer.

The comms director has set it for eight minutes because seven felt reckless and nine felt complacent.

The Minister begins.

A reporter asks whether the government is announcing faster decisions or just measuring how long the existing ones take.

The timer ticks.

The Minister glances at it.

"We are announcing that time matters."

The Permanent Secretary closes his eyes.

"And," the Minister continues, "that announcing time matters is not the same as doing something with it."

The comms director freezes.

The timer rings.

Nobody moves.

The Minister turns it off.

"There. When the bell sounds, someone has to act. Otherwise it is just noise with a handle."

A reporter asks whether the Urgency Timer programme will continue.

"Yes," says the Minister. "In kitchens, where it has an existing evidence base."

## TAG

Back in the department, the timer has been returned to Facilities.

The Permanent Secretary says it may still have value as a metaphor.

The Minister says the department has enough metaphors and not enough minutes.

The junior official enters with a note from the canteen.

The tomato is missing.

The comms director asks whether they should describe this as rapid redeployment.

From somewhere down the corridor, a bell rings.

**END**
