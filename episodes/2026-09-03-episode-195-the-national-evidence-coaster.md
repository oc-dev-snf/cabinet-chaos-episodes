# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 195
**Title:** The National Evidence Coaster
**Date:** 2026-09-03
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The Minister enters the briefing room to find every document resting on a branded coaster.

Each coaster is navy, circular, and stamped with the words:

**EVIDENCE BASE - ABSORBENT**

The Minister lifts one.

"Why is the evidence on a drink mat?"

The Permanent Secretary adjusts his glasses.

"Technically, Minister, the evidence is under a drink mat."

"That is worse."

"It depends whether you value protection or visibility."

The comms director is arranging the coasters into a camera-friendly crescent.

"The public keep asking what the policy rests on. Now we have a visual answer."

"A coaster."

"A stable platform."

The junior official arrives with a tray of water glasses and freezes.

"Are those for the evidence?"

"No," says the policy lead. "They are for the stakeholders."

"Which ones?"

"The ones who spill."

The Minister picks up a briefing note. A perfect damp ring has formed around the word "urgent".

The comms director smiles.

"Good. It shows the issue has landed."

## ACT ONE

The coaster initiative began as a modest attempt to stop reporters photographing empty tables.

An empty table looked indecisive.

A table covered in paper looked chaotic.

A table covered in paper on coasters looked, according to the presentation, "anchored".

The Minister reads the launch line.

"We are putting evidence at the heart of delivery and drinks around the perimeter."

"Strong," says comms.

"It sounds like a picnic with a governance problem."

The Permanent Secretary offers a calmer formulation.

"Evidence will be seen to support decision-making."

"Will it support decision-making?"

"That is an implementation matter."

The policy lead points to a diagram showing four coasters labelled Data, Listening, Feasibility, and Fiscal Envelope.

The Fiscal Envelope coaster is larger than the others.

"Why is finance the biggest one?"

"Because the mugs kept sliding off reality."

The junior official raises a hand.

"We have a problem with the Data coaster."

"What problem?"

"It is decorative."

There is a silence.

"All of them are decorative," says the Minister.

"Yes, but Data is also out of stock."

## ACT TWO

By midday, the department has created a Coaster Handling Protocol.

Evidence may be placed on a coaster.

Evidence may not be replaced by a coaster.

Coasters may support a glass, a mug, or a clear line to take, but not a ministerial assurance without prior clearance.

Legal adds a footnote saying absorbency is not a substitute for accountability.

Comms asks whether that can be rephrased as "absorbing lessons".

The Permanent Secretary says it cannot.

Finance says each coaster must be tagged to an outcome.

Operations says the tags have made the coasters uneven.

The Minister tries to move a glass and finds it stuck to the briefing paper beneath it.

"Why is this wet?"

"Because the stakeholder engagement water has condensed around the fiscal assumptions," says the policy lead.

"Is that a metaphor?"

"Only if it appears in the readout."

The comms director bursts in with a tablet.

"Social has noticed the coasters."

"Good or bad?"

"They are asking whether the government is literally resting policy on things designed for stains."

The Minister looks at the damp ring around "urgent" again.

"That is annoyingly fair."

## ACT THREE

The response grid fills with possible explanations.

The coasters are not props.

They are practical tools.

They are not hiding evidence.

They are foregrounding the conditions in which evidence can be safely placed.

They are not a metaphor.

They are, if necessary, a metaphor for careful stewardship.

The Minister reads the grid and puts it down.

"This is a statement defending tableware."

"It is tableware-adjacent," says the Permanent Secretary.

The junior official has been sent to retrieve actual evidence.

She returns with three reports, two contradictory datasets, and a consultation summary nobody has opened because it arrived as a locked PDF.

The policy lead looks relieved.

"Excellent. We can put those on the coasters."

"No," says the Minister. "We can read them."

The room treats this as a procedural escalation.

Comms objects first.

"Reading creates timing risk."

Finance objects second.

"Reading creates cost implications."

The Permanent Secretary objects last, and most gently.

"Reading creates a record of having read."

The Minister opens the first report.

A coaster sticks to the back page and falls onto the floor with a small, damp slap.

Nobody moves.

## CLIMAX

The press conference has been moved from the main lobby to a committee room because the main lobby table now looks like a cafe after a difficult lunch.

The Minister stands at the lectern with one coaster in his hand.

Comms has begged him not to hold it.

Legal has begged him not to call it evidence.

Finance has asked whether it can be reused.

The Permanent Secretary has advised "short, dry, and non-ceramic".

The Minister looks at the reporters.

"This morning we tried to make the evidence look tidier. That was the wrong instinct. Evidence is not tidier because you put a logo under it. It is useful when you read it, argue with it, and let it change the thing you were about to say."

A reporter asks whether the policy is being delayed.

"Yes," says the Minister. "Because the coaster was ready before the case was."

Another reporter asks what happens to the coasters.

The Minister places one under his glass.

"They can do the job they are qualified for."

## TAG

Back in the briefing room, the junior official removes the labels from the remaining coasters.

The policy lead watches the pile grow.

"Should we log them as evidence-handling assets?"

"No," she says. "They are coasters."

The Permanent Secretary considers this.

"Useful things do become harder to track once accurately described."

The junior official hands him one.

He puts his tea on it.

For the first time all day, nothing is announced.

**END**
