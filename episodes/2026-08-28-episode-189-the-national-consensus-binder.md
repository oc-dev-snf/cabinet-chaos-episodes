# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 189
**Title:** The National Consensus Binder
**Date:** 2026-08-28
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The department has decided that consensus requires rings.

Not electoral rings, or committee rings, or the sort of rings that appear under everyone's eyes after a spending review. Binder rings.

A large blue folder sits in the centre of the Cabinet table, closed with the solemnity of a national archive and the thickness of a small paving slab.

The Minister looks at it.

"Why is there a binder in my briefing?"

The Permanent Secretary smiles.

"It is the National Consensus Binder, Minister."

"That sounds like a filing error that got promoted."

The comms director says the public want government to listen, unite, and proceed without appearing either indecisive or unpleasantly decisive.

"So we bought stationery?"

"We consolidated agreement."

The policy lead opens the binder. Inside are sixteen coloured dividers, each labelled with a different form of agreement.

Practical Agreement. Emerging Agreement. Conditional Agreement. Agreement in Principle. Agreement Adjacent to Principle. Agreement Pending Tone.

The junior official says the binder is empty.

Everyone looks down.

The Permanent Secretary clears their throat.

"Yes. That is because consensus must remain capable of receiving views."

The Minister closes the binder.

"At the moment, it is receiving dust."

## ACT ONE

The National Consensus Binder began as a modest attempt to keep track of stakeholder meetings.

There had been twenty-two roundtables, eleven listening sessions, four workshops, three teach-ins, two informal fireside conversations without a fire, and one conversation described in the minutes as "constructively diagonal".

Nobody could say what had been agreed.

The junior official suggested writing it down.

The Permanent Secretary agreed, but only after the proposal was reframed as a cross-sector consensus capture mechanism.

By breakfast, procurement had found a binder.

"It needs to feel inclusive," says the comms director.

"It needs to contain decisions," says the junior official.

"Yes, but inclusively."

The policy lead presents the Binder Theory of Change.

It has four movements:

1. Open the rings.
2. Admit perspectives.
3. Close the rings.
4. Announce shared direction.

The Minister studies the slide.

"Where do we choose what to do?"

The Permanent Secretary points to movement two.

"In the admission environment."

"That is not choosing. That is putting paper in a folder."

"It is choosing to accommodate complexity."

The Minister sighs.

"Complexity is not a tenant."

## ACT TWO

The binder immediately becomes a fairness issue.

Every team wants its own divider.

Local delivery asks for an orange tab, because orange is visible without being provocative.

Finance asks whether blank pages count as unfunded commitments.

Legal asks whether closing the binder could be interpreted as closing the consultation.

Digital asks whether the binder will have an online equivalent, a dashboard equivalent, and a dashboard showing usage of the online equivalent.

The comms director proposes a launch photo in which the Minister places a sheet into the binder while saying, "We are bringing the country into the room."

"What is on the sheet?" asks the Minister.

"A representative view."

"Whose view?"

"Representation's."

The junior official suggests including the actual table of recurring points from the roundtables. It shows that most participants agreed on three small changes, disagreed on two large ones, and wanted the department to stop asking them the same question under different lighting.

The room becomes quiet.

"That is rather specific," says the Permanent Secretary.

"It is what they said."

"Yes, but it may narrow the sense of an open conversation."

"Because it answers it?"

The policy lead writes "answer risk" in the margin and underlines it twice.

## ACT THREE

Rehearsal takes place in the press room, where the binder has been placed on a lectern beside a tasteful plant and a stack of unread submissions.

The Minister is given a script.

"Today, I am placing the first page into the National Consensus Binder."

The Minister turns the page.

"What is the first page?"

The comms director checks the notes.

"A cover sheet."

"A cover sheet for what?"

"For the contents."

"Which are?"

"Forthcoming."

The junior official lifts a folder.

"I have the actual contents."

This creates immediate procedural discomfort.

The actual contents include a list of agreed practical changes: publish clearer timelines, stop changing forms mid-application, name the decision owner, and retire two duplicate reporting requests that appear to exist only because two teams once used different fonts.

The Minister brightens.

"Good. Put those in."

The Permanent Secretary hesitates.

"Minister, once those are in the binder, stakeholders may expect movement."

"They already expect movement. That is why they keep attending things with biscuits."

The comms director worries that specific actions will reduce the emotional range of the launch.

"A binder containing work feels less symbolic."

"Excellent," says the Minister. "It can have a job."

## CLIMAX

The rings jam.

No one knows why. The binder refuses to open halfway through rehearsal, trapping the cover sheet, the divider marked Emerging Agreement, and the entire shared direction of travel.

Operations brings a screwdriver.

Legal says using tools on the binder may imply process failure.

Finance asks whether repair counts as capital enhancement.

The policy lead suggests calling the jam a "consensus pause".

The Minister asks for the actual list again.

The junior official puts it on the table, outside the binder.

For the first time all morning, everyone can read it.

There are five actions, each small enough to do and clear enough to be checked. Two need ministerial sign-off. One needs finance clearance. One needs a phone call. One needs someone to admit that an old template has been pretending to be mandatory for three years.

The Minister takes a pen.

"Right. We are doing these."

The Permanent Secretary says the binder was meant to hold the consensus.

"It has. The consensus is that the binder is in the way."

The comms director looks pained, then unexpectedly nods.

"That line will test well."

By noon, three of the five actions have owners, dates, and fewer adjectives. The fourth is being cleared. The fifth is renamed from Legacy Reporting Harmony to Stop Asking Twice.

The binder remains jammed on the lectern.

It is the most honest prop the department has ever used.

## TAG

That evening, the department publishes a short update titled "What We Heard and What We Are Changing."

The National Consensus Binder is moved to a side table with a note attached:

**DO NOT OPEN UNTIL AGREEMENT HAS BEEN REACHED**
