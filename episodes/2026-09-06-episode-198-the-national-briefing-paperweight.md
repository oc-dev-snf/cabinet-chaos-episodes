# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 198
**Title:** The National Briefing Paperweight
**Date:** 2026-09-06
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The Minister arrives to find a glass paperweight sitting in the middle of the Cabinet table.

Inside it is a tiny model of a lectern, a tiny model of a folder, and a tiny model of a person looking trapped between both.

The label beneath it reads:

**NATIONAL BRIEFING PAPERWEIGHT**

The Minister stops.

"Why has the briefing been encased?"

The Permanent Secretary closes a folder with ceremonial care.

"It is a stabilisation measure."

"For paper?"

"For messages."

The comms director is polishing the paperweight with a cloth usually reserved for camera lenses.

"The public want a settled position."

"So we have trapped one in glass."

"Exactly. Transparent, weighty, and hard to misplace."

The policy lead enters carrying a tray of smaller paperweights.

"These are for subsidiary lines."

The Minister peers at them.

"This one says 'subject to detail'."

"That is our heaviest one."

The junior official arrives holding a folder that keeps springing open.

"Where should I put the live questions?"

Everyone looks at the glass object.

Nobody suggests lifting it.

## ACT ONE

The National Briefing Paperweight began as a complaint that the line kept moving.

The line had moved because the policy had moved.

The policy had moved because the figures had moved.

The figures had moved because nobody had agreed whether the old figures were still figures or had become context.

By ten o'clock, a senior adviser had written "we need weight" in a margin.

Procurement delivered three kilograms of metaphor before lunch.

The Minister reads the launch note.

"The paperweight demonstrates that government communications are grounded in clarity."

"Very solid," says comms.

"It is literally sitting on the uncertainty."

"That is one reading."

The Permanent Secretary offers the institutional version.

"It prevents premature dispersal."

"Of facts?"

"Of interpretations."

Legal has warned that the paperweight must not imply the briefing cannot be amended.

Finance has asked whether glass counts as transparency or equipment.

Operations has complained that the table now requires two people to move.

The policy lead is upbeat.

"It gives the announcement presence."

The Minister taps the paperweight.

It makes a small, expensive sound.

"It gives the announcement a concussion."

The junior official writes "avoid impact language" in the margin.

## ACT TWO

By midday the paperweight has created a hierarchy.

The main message sits under glass.

The supporting message sits under acrylic.

The regional message sits under a stapler because nobody ordered enough acrylic.

Comms insists this is useful.

"It shows which lines carry mass."

"It shows which lines are being held down," says the Minister.

The Permanent Secretary convenes a Weighting Subcommittee.

Every sentence must now be assessed for load-bearing capacity.

"We cannot have a flimsy assurance," says the policy lead.

"We cannot have a load-bearing waffle," says the Minister.

Legal says waffle is not a defined term.

Finance says if waffle is load-bearing, it may need a business case.

The junior official has been asked to produce a table showing which caveats are heavy enough to remain visible.

She returns with a grid in which "soon" is light, "shortly" is medium, and "in due course" has its own reinforced box.

The Minister points at the reinforced box.

"Why is that one so strong?"

"Because nothing has ever escaped from it."

The room accepts this with the tired respect due to a proven system.

## ACT THREE

The afternoon briefing becomes a debate about whether the paperweight should appear on camera.

Comms wants it placed beside the Minister.

"It signals seriousness."

"It signals that our position needs ballast."

"Ballast can be positive."

"Not when the ship is the policy."

The Permanent Secretary suggests a compromise.

"It could remain in shot but out of focus."

"So the public can see there is weight, but not what it is holding down."

"Precisely."

The policy lead has discovered a problem.

The latest data changes paragraph four.

Paragraph four is currently underneath the paperweight.

Nobody wants to move it because the paperweight has acquired symbolic status.

Legal advises that the paperweight can be lifted for operational purposes provided nobody describes it as a shift.

Comms proposes calling it a "controlled rebalancing".

The Minister looks at the tiny trapped lectern inside the glass.

"We are now briefing the removal of the object that was meant to prove the briefing would not move."

The junior official checks her watch.

"The lobby arrives in twelve minutes."

Everyone stares at paragraph four as if it might voluntarily become true.

## CLIMAX

In the press room, the paperweight sits just to the Minister's left.

It catches the lights beautifully.

This is immediately a problem.

The first reporter asks why the government's clearest object is being used to obscure the government's clearest paragraph.

The comms director inhales through the approved line.

The Permanent Secretary finds an urgent interest in the water jug.

The Minister looks down at the glass, then at the briefing beneath it.

"It was meant to make the message feel settled," he says. "But a message is not settled because something heavy is placed on top of it. It is settled when the facts are current, the decision is owned, and the answer survives being read without furniture."

A reporter asks whether the paperweight will remain government policy.

"No," says the Minister. "It will remain a paperweight. That is already a full-time job."

Another reporter asks what happens to paragraph four.

The Minister lifts the glass object.

The page rises with it, stuck by static.

For a moment, the whole country can see the amendment dangling underneath.

The junior official steps forward with both hands, calm as a bomb disposal expert.

## TAG

Back in the briefing room, the paperweight has been moved to a side table.

It now holds down the minutes of the meeting about whether it should have been used.

The Permanent Secretary says this is a proportionate deployment.

The Minister says nothing.

The junior official adds a sticky note to the glass.

It reads:

**DO NOT STABILISE WITHOUT CHECKING WHAT IS UNDERNEATH**

Comms photographs it.

"That could be a campaign."

The Minister leaves before anyone can procure a plinth.
