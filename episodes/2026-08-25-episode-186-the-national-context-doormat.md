# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 186
**Title:** The National Context Doormat
**Date:** 2026-08-25
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The department has discovered that people keep arriving at policy conclusions without first wiping their feet on the approved background information.

This is considered a hygiene issue.

The Minister enters the atrium to find a brand-new doormat placed in front of the briefing room door. It is navy, rectangular, and says:

**PLEASE WIPE YOUR ASSUMPTIONS**

"Why is there a doormat in a secure government corridor?"

The Permanent Secretary smiles with the careful warmth of someone introducing a budget pressure as a wellness initiative.

"It is the National Context Doormat."

"I regret asking."

"It ensures every visitor enters the discussion with sufficient context."

The comms director crouches to straighten it by two millimetres.

"It photographs as practical humility."

"It photographs as a mat."

"Yes, Minister, but a grounded one."

## ACT ONE

The National Context Doormat began when a stakeholder meeting went badly because several attendees had read only the executive summary.

The executive summary had itself been written for people who would not read the summary.

The policy team therefore commissioned a physical intervention.

"People are tired of documents," says the policy lead. "They need a journey."

"Across a doormat?"

"A short journey."

The Permanent Secretary explains that the mat is not merely decorative.

"It marks the transition from opinion to informed opinion."

"Does it actually inform anyone?"

"It invites readiness."

A junior official suggests putting the key facts on the wall beside it.

There is a silence.

The comms director frowns.

"Facts on walls can look accusatory."

"Only to people walking past them," says the junior official.

The Minister asks what happens if someone refuses to wipe their assumptions.

The Permanent Secretary says they will be "supported toward contextual compliance".

## ACT TWO

By lunchtime, the doormat has become a process.

Security asks whether visitors need to step on it once or twice.

Facilities asks whether context is machine washable.

Finance asks whether a doormat can be classified as public engagement infrastructure.

Legal wants to know whether "wipe your assumptions" implies that citizens arrive dirty.

The comms director proposes a softer version:

**THANK YOU FOR ENTERING WITH BALANCED PERSPECTIVE**

The Minister says that sounds less like a mat and more like a hostage note from a seminar.

The policy lead unveils a draft Context Entry Protocol.

It requires every meeting attendee to pause on the mat, state one thing they do not yet know, and accept that the agenda may contain nuance.

"That is going to add ten minutes to every meeting."

"Only the meetings with assumptions."

"So every meeting."

The junior official asks whether they could send a clear one-page briefing the day before instead.

The room treats this as an impressive but culturally impossible suggestion.

## ACT THREE

The first formal trial takes place before a meeting on a delayed national programme that has been announced three times, paused twice, and described as "accelerating" throughout.

The Minister stands inside the room.

The Permanent Secretary stands beside the mat with a clipboard.

The comms director has arranged for a discreet photographer to capture "the moment context lands".

The first stakeholder approaches.

"Do I have to stand on this?"

"Only if you wish to participate from a position of shared understanding," says the Permanent Secretary.

The stakeholder steps on it.

"Fine. I don't know why the latest timetable excludes the regional data."

The room freezes.

The second stakeholder steps on it.

"I don't know who owns the risk register."

The third says:

"I don't know why the published dashboard says green when the delivery note says amber-red."

The Minister slowly turns to the policy lead.

"Is the doormat making people ask useful questions?"

The comms director whispers, "We may have over-enabled context."

## CLIMAX

Within twenty minutes, the meeting has become dangerously informative.

The mat produces a queue.

Every person who steps on it volunteers a gap, an uncertainty, or a piece of missing evidence.

The junior official begins writing them on a whiteboard.

Soon the board contains twelve real issues, six named owners, and one action that can be completed by Friday if nobody adds a steering group.

The Permanent Secretary tries to restore calm.

"We must be careful not to confuse context with momentum."

"I would enjoy that confusion," says the Minister.

The comms director is worried that the photos show people looking thoughtful.

"Thoughtful can read as troubled."

"They are troubled because they have information."

"Exactly."

The policy lead suggests replacing the doormat with a Context Coaster, which would be less operationally provocative.

The Minister refuses.

"No. For once, the prop is doing the work."

The junior official points to the board.

"The work is doing the work."

"Even better. Keep both."

## TAG

By evening, the department has issued a short update listing the twelve questions raised and the first three actions.

It is the clearest thing the programme has published all year.

The doormat is moved outside the main meeting room.

The Permanent Secretary commissions guidance on appropriate assumption-wiping behaviour.

The comms director asks whether the slogan can be made more reassuring.

The Minister looks at the mat.

"No. It is the first honest sentence we've owned."

The junior official arrives with a new one-page briefing.

It has facts, dates, risks, and no slogan.

The Minister places it on the table.

"Excellent. Put this where people can read it."

The Permanent Secretary gestures toward the wall.

"As a contextual display?"

"As paper."

The doormat remains in place.

By the next morning, someone has added a small handwritten note underneath:

**AND CHECK YOUR SHOES FOR DELIVERY CLAIMS**

**END**
