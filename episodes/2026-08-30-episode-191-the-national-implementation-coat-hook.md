# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 191
**Title:** The National Implementation Coat Hook
**Date:** 2026-08-30
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The department has installed a coat hook outside the Cabinet room.

It is brass, polished, and mounted on a square of oak with the quiet authority of something that arrived before anyone could ask why. A small label beneath it reads:

**HANG IMPLEMENTATION HERE**

The Minister stops mid-stride.

"Why is there a coat hook for implementation?"

The Permanent Secretary looks pleased in the way senior officials look pleased when a metaphor has survived procurement.

"It is the National Implementation Coat Hook, Minister."

"Implementation is not a coat."

"No. But it is often left in corridors."

The comms director circles it, assessing the line of sight from the press room.

"It photographs well. Practical, modest, purposeful. A hook says delivery without overclaiming delivery."

The junior official checks the wall.

"There is nothing on it."

"That," says the Permanent Secretary, "is because we have not yet agreed what implementation is wearing."

## ACT ONE

The hook was commissioned after a review found that several major initiatives had been launched with admirable ambition but nowhere obvious to put the bit where things actually happened.

The review recommended a "visible point of operational attachment".

Facilities recommended a shelf.

Comms preferred a hook.

Policy asked whether attachment could be defined in phases.

The Minister is shown a slide titled From Announcement To Hook: A Journey.

"This is twelve pages about a wall fitting."

"It is eleven pages about confidence," says the policy lead. "The final page is about load-bearing."

The hook has been rated for scarves, lanyards, light coats, and medium strategic expectations.

"What happens if the expectation is heavy?"

The Permanent Secretary folds his hands.

"We spread it across multiple hooks."

"That is not delivery. That is cloakroom management."

The comms director makes a note.

"Cloakroom management could test well as a phrase for joined-up government."

## ACT TWO

By mid-morning, every team has proposed something to hang from the hook.

Delivery brings a laminated timetable, but the string snaps under the weight of footnotes.

Finance brings an envelope marked Funding Envelope, which cannot be opened because it is awaiting a separate envelope.

Legal brings a disclaimer on a ribbon.

Digital brings a QR code to a page saying the implementation plan will be available shortly.

The Minister stares at the collection.

"Why is the hook carrying everything except a plan?"

The policy lead objects mildly.

"There is a plan. It is currently being consolidated into a planning posture."

"A posture is what you have when you are trying to look like you have a plan."

The Permanent Secretary suggests that the hook has already performed a useful function by surfacing dependencies.

"The dependencies were already surfaced. They were in the email marked dependencies."

"Yes, but now they have presence."

The junior official lifts the empty coat hanger.

"Could we hang a list of decisions still needed?"

The room goes quiet.

"That might imply decisions are still needed," says comms.

"They are."

"Exactly. We must avoid unnecessary implication through accuracy."

## ACT THREE

The hook becomes the centrepiece of the afternoon readiness session.

A photographer has been booked for three minutes, subject to clearance, weather, and whether the hook can be described as "new" if it was installed before lunch.

The Minister rehearses beside it.

"Today we are setting out a practical route to delivery..."

The comms director raises a hand.

"Can we say route if there is not yet a map?"

"Fine. A practical direction."

Legal leans forward.

"Direction may imply enforceability."

"A practical sense of travel?"

The Permanent Secretary nods.

"That preserves movement while avoiding arrival."

The junior official places three sheets on the table: the launch statement, the operational checklist, and the unresolved decisions log.

"The statement says implementation starts immediately. The checklist says mobilisation starts next month. The decisions log says we have not chosen who owns the first step."

The Minister looks from the papers to the hook.

"So the hook is ready before the programme."

"Readiness is encouraging," says the policy lead.

"Readiness without ownership is furniture."

## CLIMAX

An emergency ownership meeting is called around the hook.

Every department agrees the first step is essential, cross-cutting, and better delivered by someone with the appropriate levers.

No one admits to having levers.

The Permanent Secretary proposes a "distributed ownership model", which consists of everyone initialling the same page in different pens.

The junior official clears a space and writes four questions on a plain sheet:

**WHO DOES THE FIRST THING?**

**BY WHEN?**

**WITH WHAT MONEY?**

**WHO SAYS IF IT HAS HAPPENED?**

The comms director winces.

"That is quite stark."

"It is four legs on a chair," says the Minister. "We have been polishing the coat hook."

The room studies the questions with the cautious respect usually reserved for exposed wiring.

Delivery names an owner.

Finance names a budget line.

Legal names the decision that must be recorded.

The Permanent Secretary names a meeting to confirm the naming, then sees the Minister's expression and downgrades it to a ten-minute call.

The sheet is hung from the hook.

It does not photograph as well as the brass.

It is, however, heavier.

## TAG

The launch is delayed by two hours and improved by four sentences.

The final announcement says what starts today, what starts next month, who owns the first step, and what will be published when it is done.

The comms director describes the reaction as "unhelpfully comprehensible".

By evening, the coat hook has acquired a second label in permanent marker:

**A HOOK IS NOT A HANDOVER**

Underneath, the junior official has added:

**BUT IT CAN HOLD ONE**
