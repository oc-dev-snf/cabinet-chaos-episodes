# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 188
**Title:** The National Delivery Paperclip
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The department has decided that delivery needs holding together.

This is why there is a paperclip on the Cabinet table.

Not a large paperclip. Not a historic paperclip. An ordinary silver paperclip, placed under a glass dome as though it once belonged to a saint of administration.

The Minister enters the room and stops.

"Why is office stationery being preserved for the nation?"

The Permanent Secretary smiles.

"It is the National Delivery Paperclip, Minister."

"That is not an answer. That is an object wearing a job title."

The comms director says the public are tired of plans, frameworks, roadmaps, dashboards, and action trackers.

"So we're giving them a paperclip?"

"We're giving them a symbol of grip."

"A paperclip has no grip. It has tension."

The policy lead nods as if the Minister has accidentally improved the strategy.

"Exactly. Delivery through constructive tension."

The junior official points out that the paperclip is currently holding nothing together.

The room falls silent.

The Permanent Secretary carefully slides two blank sheets into it.

"There. A programme."

## ACT ONE

The National Delivery Paperclip began in a note about departmental clutter.

The original note said that no one could find the delivery plan because it had been filed behind the delivery plan refresh, the refreshed delivery plan tracker, and the tracker reset summary.

By the time it reached senior officials, the problem had been reclassified as insufficient narrative cohesion.

"We need something simple," says the comms director.

"We need the actual delivery plan," says the junior official.

"Yes, but simple."

The policy lead presents the Paperclip Operating Model.

It has five principles:

1. Gather loose commitments.
2. Align their edges.
3. Apply gentle pressure.
4. Avoid puncturing the page.
5. Announce coherence.

The Minister stares at the screen.

"Where does doing things appear?"

The Permanent Secretary gestures at principle three.

"Within the pressure environment."

"That could mean anything."

"Precisely why it can apply across government."

The paperclip is then photographed beside a fountain pen, a closed laptop, and a mug that says DELIVERY CULTURE.

Nobody asks who owns the mug.

## ACT TWO

The paperclip immediately creates implementation difficulties.

Legal asks whether clipped commitments are more binding than stapled commitments.

Finance asks whether the paperclip can be reused between programmes without double-counting its impact.

Digital asks whether every paperclip requires a unique identifier.

Operations asks whether bulldog clips are excluded for ideological reasons.

The comms director proposes a launch image in which the Minister clips together a stack of priority documents while saying, "We are joining up delivery."

"Are the documents real?" asks the Minister.

"They are representative."

"Representative of what?"

"Documents."

The junior official offers to print the real list of stalled commitments, including owners, blockers, and dates.

The Permanent Secretary says real lists can create a misleading impression that progress depends on specific people doing specific work by specific times.

"Does it not?"

"Not in the round."

The policy lead writes "not in the round" beside an arrow pointing to the paperclip.

## ACT THREE

Rehearsal takes place in the media room, where the paperclip has been enlarged on a backdrop until it resembles critical infrastructure.

The Minister stands at a lectern with three documents.

Document one is called THE PLAN.

Document two is called THE DELIVERY OF THE PLAN.

Document three is called THE PLAN FOR DELIVERY OF THE DELIVERY PLAN.

"Why do we have three plans?" asks the Minister.

The comms director says three looks intentional.

"Two looks thin. Four looks like trouble."

The Minister clips them together.

The paperclip pings off the papers, strikes the lectern microphone, and produces a noise that the broadcast team describes as "unhelpfully democratic."

The junior official picks up the loose pages.

"The problem is these documents contradict each other."

"Can we clip them more firmly?" asks the policy lead.

"No. Page one says decisions will be local. Page two says decisions require central clearance. Page three says decisions should be paused until the delivery board decides how decisions are decided."

The Minister looks at the Permanent Secretary.

"So the paperclip has failed because the policy is unclippable."

"It has exposed an alignment maturity issue."

"It has exposed a mess."

The room considers this.

Nobody can find a softer synonym quickly enough.

## CLIMAX

The Minister cancels the launch for twenty minutes and orders everyone into a working session.

At first, the officials try to preserve the paperclip theme.

The comms director suggests calling the meeting a "clip sprint."

The policy lead suggests "coherence capture."

The Permanent Secretary suggests "a focused consolidation moment."

The Minister suggests shutting the door.

Once the door is shut, the junior official reads out every contradictory instruction in the three documents. There are sixteen.

Local teams cannot proceed without central approval, central approval cannot be granted without local evidence, and the evidence template has been withdrawn pending a review of whether evidence feels accessible.

The Minister takes a pen.

"Right. Cross out anything that stops the work but does not protect anyone from harm, waste, or illegality."

The Permanent Secretary looks physically pained by the efficiency.

"Minister, process exists for a reason."

"Good. Then the useless bits won't mind being asked what theirs is."

By lunch, the three plans have become one document with owners, dates, and a page titled "Things We Will Stop Pretending Are Dependencies."

The paperclip finally holds the pages together.

It is no longer a symbol.

It is doing stationery.

## TAG

That evening, the department publishes the revised delivery plan without the launch video.

The comms director quietly keeps one photo of the paperclip under glass for future emergencies.

The junior official replaces the empty display case with a note:

**CURRENTLY IN USE**
