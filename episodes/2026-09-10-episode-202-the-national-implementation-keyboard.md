# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 202
**Title:** The National Implementation Keyboard
**Date:** 2026-09-10
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The Minister enters the Cabinet room to find a keyboard in the centre of the table.

It is not connected to anything.

Every key has been relabelled.

**DELIVER**

**ALIGN**

**REASSURE**

**RESET**

The space bar says **FISCAL HEADROOM**.

"Why is there a computer part in the middle of government?"

The Permanent Secretary looks pleased in the restrained way of someone unveiling a car park.

"It is the National Implementation Keyboard."

"That sounds like a device for typing things that do not happen."

The comms director circles it with her phone raised.

"It photographs as decisive."

"It photographs as a cry for technical support."

The policy lead taps **DELIVER**.

Nothing happens.

Everyone waits.

The junior official checks the underside.

"It needs batteries."

The Permanent Secretary nods.

"That is phase two."

## ACT ONE

The keyboard was commissioned after a review found that ministers were pressing for action without having a shared understanding of what pressing meant.

By nine o'clock it had a user guide.

By half past nine it had a steering group.

By ten it had generated an accessibility concern because the **ESC** key had been renamed **MANAGED TRANSITION**.

The Minister reads the briefing.

"The keyboard creates a tactile interface between aspiration and implementation."

"It is a prop," says the junior official.

"A prop can support delivery," says the policy lead.

"So can a chair, but we do not ask it to run adult social care."

Legal is worried the **ENTER** key could be interpreted as formal approval.

Finance is worried the **SHIFT** key could imply a change in spending priorities.

Comms is worried that the public may ask why **BACKSPACE** is worn out.

"Can we call it a legacy correction button?" asks the comms director.

"No," says the Minister. "We are not rebranding deletion as heritage."

The Permanent Secretary suggests a dry run.

The Minister presses **CLARIFY**.

The lights flicker.

The policy lead says that may be coincidence.

Facilities says it may be procurement.

## ACT TWO

Departments begin requesting custom keys.

Transport wants **CONSULT LOCALLY** in high visibility yellow.

Health wants a larger **PRIORITISE** key, ideally with a waiting-room queue management function.

Education asks whether **LEARN LESSONS** can be made child-safe.

Treasury sends a note saying it supports the keyboard in principle, provided nobody presses anything with fiscal, legal, temporal, moral, or operational implications.

The Permanent Secretary convenes the Keystroke Assurance Board.

The Minister looks at the first agenda item.

"Define whether a press is a decision, an intention, a signal, or an emotional event."

"We need consistency," says the policy lead.

"We need a government that can tell the difference between a button and a plan."

The comms director proposes a slogan: **Pressing Ahead**.

Legal says that may imply ahead exists.

Finance says ahead may require costings.

The junior official points to a key in the corner.

"What does **ANY** do?"

Everyone looks at it.

The Permanent Secretary says it is for general situations.

"So when the system says press any key, we press that?"

"Only after clearance."

The Minister rests his forehead gently on the table.

This accidentally presses **REVIEW**.

Three officials enter carrying folders.

## ACT THREE

By lunchtime the keyboard has become a compliance mechanism.

No one is allowed to write "we will do this" unless a corresponding key has been pressed, witnessed, logged, and quality-assured by someone wearing a visitor pass.

The corridor outside fills with officials asking whether **TAB** counts as cross-departmental movement.

The policy lead unveils a keystroke dashboard.

Green means a key has been pressed.

Amber means a key has been pressed but not believed.

Red means a key has been pressed by the wrong hand.

Grey means the key is awaiting a narrative.

The Minister studies the dashboard.

"There is no column for anything actually happening."

"That is being scoped," says the Permanent Secretary.

The junior official finds a cable in the box.

"Should we plug it in?"

The room becomes extremely quiet.

Comms says a connected keyboard changes the optics.

Legal says a connected keyboard changes liability.

Finance says connection creates expectations.

The Minister says that, in his experience, expectations are what happens when citizens notice government has electricity.

He plugs it in.

On the screen at the end of the room, a blank document appears.

The title reads:

**IMPLEMENTATION PLAN**

The cursor blinks.

Nobody moves.

## CLIMAX

In the press room, the keyboard sits beside the lectern under a cloth that does not hide its shape.

A reporter asks whether the government has replaced delivery with symbolic typing.

The comms director gives the encouraging smile of a person trying to make a loading bar look patriotic.

The Permanent Secretary holds a folder labelled **KEYBOARD LINE TO TAKE**.

The Minister ignores it.

"A key is not implementation. A dashboard is not implementation. A phrase is not implementation. Implementation is naming the work, naming the owner, naming the deadline, and then accepting that the public will judge the result, not the font on the announcement."

The junior official brings him the connected keyboard.

The Minister types one sentence into the blank plan.

**By Friday, publish the three things we will do first and the five things we will stop pretending are first.**

He presses **ENTER**.

This time, something happens.

The printer wakes up.

Everyone looks terrified.

## TAG

Back in the Cabinet room, the keyboard has been returned to ordinary letters.

The Minister types slowly.

"Done means done."

The junior official peers over.

"You missed an e."

The Minister looks at the screen.

It says:

"Don means done."

The Permanent Secretary enters.

"We can work with that."

**END**
