# THE THICK OF ITCH

**Episode:** 209
**Title:** The National Briefing Lanyard
**Date:** 2026-09-17
**Genre:** Original political satire

---

## COLD OPEN

The Minister arrives at the morning grid meeting wearing three lanyards.

One says VISITOR.

One says SPEAKER.

One says PLEASE RETURN TO SECURITY.

"Why am I dressed like a conference that lost a fight?"

The Permanent Secretary studies the arrangement with professional calm.

"It is the National Briefing Lanyard."

"It is around my neck."

"Precisely. The message is close to the decision-maker."

The comms director explains that the lanyard is a portable symbol of access, accountability, and mild fabric-based seriousness.

"People trust a person with a pass."

"People trust them to get through a door."

"That is already more than most policy achieves by breakfast."

The policy lead hands the Minister a laminated card clipped to a fresh strap.

Across the top, in discreet official type, it says:

**ACCESS TO DELIVERY.**

The Minister turns it over.

The reverse side says:

**DELIVERY TO ACCESS.**

"Which side is policy?"

"Whichever is facing the camera."

## ACT ONE

The lanyard began as an event badge for a stakeholder roundtable.

By ten, it had become a whole-of-government identity framework.

The Minister reads the submission.

"Departments are invited to align visible credentials with mission-led outcomes."

"It means," says the Permanent Secretary, "that everyone should look entitled to be in the room."

"Are they entitled to be in the room?"

"That is being handled by Facilities."

The comms director has prepared a hierarchy of straps.

Blue means delivery.

Green means listening.

Grey means fiscal discipline.

Red means urgent cross-cutting grip.

The Minister points at a striped one.

"What is that?"

"Listening to urgent cross-cutting fiscal delivery."

"That is four meetings and a migraine."

Legal asks whether a lanyard marked ACCESS could imply access to actual services.

The policy lead suggests adding an asterisk.

The Permanent Secretary says the public dislike asterisks unless they are on savings.

The comms director proposes calling it "bounded access".

"So no access."

"Access with a perimeter."

## ACT TWO

Departments begin requesting bespoke lanyards.

Transport wants one that works during disruption.

Health asks for wipe-clean versions for symbolic resilience.

Education suggests child-sized stakeholder badges, then immediately withdraws the phrase child-sized stakeholders.

The Treasury asks whether ministers can share one lanyard between appearances.

The policy lead unveils the Credential Confidence Matrix.

Tier one is wearing the lanyard.

Tier two is tapping it against a reader.

Tier three is being waved through by someone who looks uncertain.

Tier four is entering a restricted area while saying "I'm expected".

The Minister frowns.

"That is not delivery. That is theatre with doors."

"Doors test implementation," says the Permanent Secretary.

An alert arrives from the afternoon site visit.

The prototype reader has rejected the Secretary of State's badge.

The comms director asks whether anyone filmed it.

"Several people."

"Then it is a pilot."

The junior official notes that the system may have mistaken the Secretary of State for an external stakeholder.

Nobody speaks for a moment.

The Minister says, "Could it do that again, but constitutionally?"

## ACT THREE

An emergency credentials board is convened.

No one can enter because the room has been set to invite-only.

They meet in the corridor under a fire notice.

The Permanent Secretary says this gives the discussion an appropriately operational feel.

The comms director wants a new line: the government is not introducing barriers, it is demonstrating managed entry.

"Managed entry to what?"

"Progress."

"Where is progress located?"

"That is why we need the map."

The policy lead produces a floor plan showing zones of participation.

Zone A is decision.

Zone B is assurance.

Zone C is refreshments.

The Minister notices Zone C is larger than Zone A.

"Why does the biscuit table have more democratic space than the cabinet subcommittee?"

"Because people can find it."

Facilities reports that several staff have swapped lanyards to attend a better catered meeting.

The Permanent Secretary calls this an informal mobility pathway.

The Minister calls it lunch.

For once, both descriptions are accurate.

## CLIMAX

The afternoon announcement is staged at a civic hub with an automatic gate.

Behind the Minister, officials wear colour-coded lanyards and stand in calibrated clusters of access.

A reporter asks whether the scheme gives people more influence or simply more things around their necks.

The comms director makes a tiny lowering motion, meaning "sincerity".

The Minister taps his lanyard against the reader.

The gate flashes red.

He taps again.

Red.

The junior official whispers that the Minister's pass is only valid for observation.

"Observation of what?"

"Delivery, Minister."

The reporters lean in.

The Minister looks at the blocked gate, then at the public waiting on the other side.

"Then let us observe the obvious. A badge is not access if the door stays shut."

The gate opens for a cleaner carrying a tray of mugs.

The Minister steps aside.

"There. Someone with a real job has priority."

The comms director writes down "human-centred access moment" before anyone can stop her.

## TAG

Back at the department, the lanyards are collected for evaluation.

The Permanent Secretary says the pilot has proved that visible credentials can support a culture of managed participation.

The Minister says it proved his own government would not let him into a room he was announcing.

The junior official holds up the rejected pass.

Someone has added a sticker:

**ACCESS PENDING DELIVERY.**

The comms director asks whether that is available in grey.

**END**
